\begin{abstract}
Reported AUCs for AI-generated image detection are often taken as evidence that detectors have learned generation-specific traces. We audit a simpler alternative: many detectors may be learning benchmark shortcuts instead. Across CIFAKE, Defactify, and GenImage, we first measure construction artifacts in the data. CIFAKE is artifact-controlled: both real and fake images are uniform $32\times32$ JPEG. Defactify has strong resolution bias but no format bias. GenImage has both: real images are JPEG photographs with diverse sizes, while fake images are PNG outputs at canonical generator resolutions. These differences produce sharply different high-frequency spectral divergences between real and fake splits: $0.34$ for CIFAKE, $4.23$ for Defactify, and $6.16$ for GenImage.

We then evaluate six public detectors plus a simple spectral CNN. The key finding is inversion on the clean benchmark: all six external detectors score below chance on CIFAKE (AUC $0.29$--$0.50$), which indicates that they are not merely failing out of distribution but often applying the wrong rule with confidence. The same detectors look strong again when the shortcut reappears. On GenImage, FreqNet reaches $0.977$ AUC, NPR reaches $0.951$, and UnivFD reaches $0.961$, despite failing on CIFAKE. This pattern is consistent with benchmark reward hacking: success tracks artifact availability more closely than generator identity. A simple normalization diagnostic further separates detectors that remain shortcut-bound from B-Free, which improves from $0.497$ to $0.637$ on CIFAKE after re-encoding. Together, these results argue that artifact audits and controlled counter-benchmark evaluations should be standard before claiming that a detector has learned genuine generative evidence.
\end{abstract}
