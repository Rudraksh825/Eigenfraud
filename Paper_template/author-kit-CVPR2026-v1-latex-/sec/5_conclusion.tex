\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\paragraph{What the results show.}
Across three benchmarks, a purely frequency-domain CNN with no pretrained weights, no semantic features, and no data augmentation can separate real from AI-generated images well above chance. On a single-generator benchmark (CIFAKE), the isotropic and full 2D representations perform within 0.013 AUC of each other. On a multi-generator mixture (Defactify), the gap grows to 0.061 AUC in favour of the 2D model. On a four-generator subset of GenImage, the 2D model achieves near-perfect in-distribution AUC, though this likely reflects the well-documented JPEG quality bias in that dataset~\cite{grommelt2024} as much as genuine detection ability.

The direction of the results is consistent: as the generator pool becomes more diverse, the full 2D representation pulls ahead of the radial average. This is what you would expect if different generators have different anisotropic fingerprints that azimuthal averaging washes out. The result is not surprising in hindsight, but it is useful to have a clean empirical measurement of the gap rather than just an intuition.

\paragraph{What this project does not show.}
We did not run cross-generator generalisation experiments (training on some generators and testing on unseen ones), which is the harder and more interesting evaluation. We did not control for JPEG quality on GenImage, so the 0.999 AUC there does not tell us much about the detector's actual ability to detect those specific generators. We did not evaluate on the full eight-generator GenImage benchmark --- only four generators were available due to extraction constraints. And we have not tested adversarial robustness: a frequency-only detector operating on a known, fixed transform is a natural target for spectral perturbation attacks~\cite{madry2018pgd,carlini2020evading}.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The method is restricted to whole-image classification and cannot localise a manipulated region. It is also blind to semantic implausibility --- the wrong number of fingers, garbled text --- that a foundation-model-based detector would flag. More fundamentally, the Fourier transform is a global operation, so the detector is sensitive to any frequency-domain signal in the full image, including image-level metadata like compression quality. Disentangling these is important but was outside the scope of this project.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
Frequency-domain detection still works in the diffusion era, at least for in-distribution evaluation. The main takeaway from this project is that the choice between the isotropic (1D) and full (2D) representation is not purely an engineering trade-off: it is a scientifically meaningful axis, and the gap between them grows with generator diversity. The architectures are small and reproducible, the preprocessing is a handful of lines of numpy, and the full pipeline runs on a single GPU. For a class project, that is probably enough to make the point; for anything more serious, the next steps are obvious: JPEG-matched evaluation, leave-one-generator-out generalisation, and robustness to targeted spectral perturbations.
